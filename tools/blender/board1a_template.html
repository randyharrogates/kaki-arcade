Design gate · board 1A · modelling approach

How the cooks get made

Three choices that come before the art style board. Every picture here is a real Blender render made by script on your Mac, or a real downloaded model. They are first-pass models to show the approach, not final art: the style board that follows adds faces, clothing detail, props and the stall.

Reply with one from each
  1. A How stylised: A1, A2 or A3
  2. B Where models come from: B1, B2 or B3
  3. C How many characters: C1, C2, C3 or C4
Mixing is fine, for example “A1 heads on A2 bodies”.
Choice A

How stylised should the cooks be?

The same cook (cap, yellow shirt, red apron) built three ways, in the same light, at the same scale. The look does not change frame rate: every option ends up as flat pre-rendered sprites, so all three draw at the same speed. What changes is how well a cook reads from the sofa and how reliably I can animate it.

A1

Chunky toy

Two heads tall, glossy vinyl-toy finish.

  • The head is half the sprite, so face, hair and hat read from 3 m.
  • Short limbs make walk cycles clean with no foot sliding.
  • Arms are short, so chopping and carrying are less expressive.
  • Gloss can look plasticky; it can be toned down.
A2

Soft clay

Two and a half heads tall, matte hand-made finish.

  • Longer arms and legs show chop, stir and carry clearly.
  • Matte clay suits a nostalgic kopitiam mood.
  • The head is smaller on the TV than A1.
  • Legs are long enough to need knees: a little more animation work.
Recommended, or a mix of A1 and A2
A3

Realistic proportions

Seven heads tall, lightly textured cloth.

  • Most grown-up look.
  • At game size the head is about 20 px: you cannot tell who is who.
  • Scripted anatomy reads as a mannequin. Faces and hands are where it shows.
  • Realistic walking needs weight shift, knees and elbows: the highest risk to “correct movement”.

At real game size

A full 1920 × 1080 frame with 8 cooks, 120 px per floor tile, nothing scrolling. Open this page on the TV to judge it from the sofa. The kitchen is a grey placeholder.

Choice B

Where do the models come from?

This is the fallback question: what I am allowed to reach for if scripted models do not meet your bar.

B1

Scripted by Claude

Everything in choice A was made this way.

  • Original: nobody else has these characters.
  • Free, and every asset rebuilds from a script.
  • I control the rig, so I can author chop, stir, wash and throw.
  • Works for stylised looks only; fine sculpted detail is out of reach.
Recommended
B2

Public-domain base models

Kenney “Mini Characters” (CC0), shown as downloaded, in our lighting.

  • Free. Comes rigged with walk, pick-up and holding animations.
  • No chop, stir or wash: I would still author those.
  • Other games use the same models, so the look is not ours. That sits badly with the originality rule.
  • The blocky style is fixed; turning these into hawker cooks is real rework.
B3

Paid AI 3D generation

No sample here: making one needs a paid account, and spending money is your call.

  • Detailed, sculpted-looking meshes from a text prompt or a drawing.
  • Messy geometry that is hard to rig, so animation quality is a gamble.
  • Hard to keep several characters in one consistent style.
  • A subscription cost, and not reproducible from a script.

If you want to see this before deciding, say so and I will check current prices first.

Choice C

How many different characters?

You asked for several. Each fully separate character needs its own complete set of animation frames, and those frames sit in the big screen’s memory for the whole round. The budget for a round is 256 MB (PERF-024), and your TV has far less to spare than the MacBook.

C3 / C4

Fully distinct characters

Different bodies, clothes and heads: uncle, auntie, young cook, makcik. The apron carries the player colour.

C2

One body, different heads

The same four heads on one shared body. Only the head is drawn per character, so memory barely grows.
